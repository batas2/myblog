Two services on one platform solved almost the same problem. One was built properly: clean boundaries, a published contract, a model you could read cold. The other was a lump.

Four teams integrated with the lump.

For a while I explained to myself why that was the organization's failure. It was not. The lump sat closer to what people needed on the day they needed it, and no design review was going to close that gap.

So this is the measure I use now. An architecture is worth what other teams are willing to depend on. Not what it could support one day. What is plugged in this week.

Which makes adoption an input to the design, not a phase that arrives later and happens to you.

Things spread inside a company on two engines, and the two run on different fuel.

Pull. Somebody has the problem today, finds your thing, and calling it costs less than building their own. It grows on its own and each new adopter is close to free. It also cannot be scheduled, which is exactly why programme managers are right to be nervous about it.

Push. Somebody decides that everyone will use the thing. It is the only engine that hits a date. It also spends roadmap slots inside other people's teams and goodwill that comes back slowly, and the bigger the change, the faster that bill grows.

Here is where I disagree with almost everyone.

Platform engineering's consensus is close to unanimous: build the platform as a product, win teams over with golden paths, never mandate. I think that is right about the product and wrong about the window.

Lay the two engines over Wardley's evolution axis. In genesis a mandate buys compliance without adoption, plus a long tail of adapters written by irritated people. At commodity it is unnecessary, because nobody mandates the company database. Between those two ends sits a stretch where the thing is good enough to adopt and not yet obviously worth adopting. That is the only place a mandate pays for itself, and most internal platform work lives right there.

Inside that window the usual objection loses most of its force. A quarter of mandated use turns into a dependency nobody wants to remove, and a dependency nobody wants to remove is adoption, whatever it was called on the day it arrived.

The window has a ceiling and I cannot compute where it sits. What I can recognise now is the feeling of being past it: another quarter of programme management, four teams left, none of them moving.

If you have a number for that ceiling, I would rather have it than be agreed with.
